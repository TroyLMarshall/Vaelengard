**The Marl Coast** is the name given to the wider stretch of country surrounding Port Ancelin — chalky downland, river valleys, tidal flats, and old forest, none of it especially fertile on its own, but together forming the patchwork of land and trade that keeps the town alive. The name comes from the marl and chalk soil that dominates the Wold and much of the coastline — poor ground for most farming, but exactly the kind of thin, well-drained earth sheep thrive on, and the geological thread that ties the region's identity together even where its industries differ wildly.

## **Regions**
- **[[Port Ancelin]]** — the harbour town itself, where the coast's various trades converge, get weighed, and leave by ship.
- **[[The Wold]]** — chalk downland inland from town, sheep country, home to the Kestrel and other flockmaster families and the smallholders who resent them.
- **[[The Greenshaw]]** — old managed forest further inland, source of the timber and shipwright knowledge behind every hull and mast at Varnay Quay.
- **[[The Greywick Saltings]]** — coastal tidal flats along the Marl Coast, salt-pan country, held largely by the insular Corbett family.
- **[[The Lowfield]]** — fertile farmland south of the Wold, the region's breadbasket, worked under the older manor system of the Averlyn family.

## **A Regional Identity, Not a Political One**
"The Marl Coast" isn't a title of governance — there's no Marl Coast lord, charter, or council. It's closer to how the region is spoken of by outsiders and traders than how any single authority within it would describe itself; a Wold shepherd and a Greenshaw shipwright might never think to use the term for their own home, but a Vos family factor in Bruges — or any foreign merchant sizing up where a shipment actually came from — uses it as convenient shorthand for "the wool coast, and everything that feeds it." Port Ancelin, and by extension Ancelin Hall, is the closest thing the region has to a unifying seat, if only because every road, river, and trade route in it eventually funnels through the town's harbour.

## **What Ties It Together**
Beyond the soil, the Marl Coast's regions share a common structural tension that plays out differently in each: a dominant, comfortable family or institution sitting atop the trade (the Kestrels, the Corbetts, the Averlyns, the Wrightmoot, the de Varnays themselves) and a less secure population beneath them who bear the real risk when a season goes wrong. The Mallow Affair is the clearest example anyone in Port Ancelin can point to of what happens when that imbalance goes unchecked — and every other region of the Marl Coast has its own quieter version of the same fault line, waiting for its own Robin Attwood to notice.
